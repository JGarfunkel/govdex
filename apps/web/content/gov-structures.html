GovDex: Understanding US Government Structures

Introduction

Government below the federal level is hard to read. A resident who wants to know who decides a bus route, a school budget or a zoning change often has to do some research. The names of officials are not well known either (80% of Americans cannot name their state legislators, per a Johns Hopkins survey).

That difficulty has a cost. When the structure is unclear, ordinary people do not know where to show up. Professionals do know. Lobbyists, consultants, trade associations and full-time advocates make it their job to know which body decides what, who sits on it and when it meets. Their clients get heard. Everyone else delegates the work or stays out.

A body that no one can locate is also a body that few people scrutinize. Responsibility spreads across several bodies, and each can point to another.

The differences between states further complicate this. Louisiana’s parishes and Nebraska’s single-chamber legislature are easy to spot and easy to remember. The harder differences are how school districts are formed and governed, what “strong mayor” means in a given charter, and which bodies appoint, fund and review the agencies that run a service such as transit. These vary by state and often by county.

GovDex exists to make that structure visible, by address and by topic.

The basic model

The simple model of American government below the federal level has three layers: state, county and city. Most people carry that model, and forms reinforce it with a line for each.

The model breaks down at “city.” When a form asks for your city, it is asking for your mailing address, and the post office draws its lines differently than government does. A Brooklyn or Flushing address is New York City. A Levittown address is in the Town of Hempstead, and Levittown is a hamlet with no government of its own. In New York the government closest to you may be a city, a town, or a village inside a town. In other states it may be a borough or a township. Where your mail goes and where you are governed can be two different places.

What people learn about government

Most Americans carry a working model of the federal government. They know a president can veto a bill, that Congress can override with a two-thirds vote, and that ending a Senate filibuster takes 60 votes. School civics and national news repeat these rules until they stick.

State government gets far less attention. Few residents can say how their state budget is made or who holds which power in the legislature. In New York the budget is negotiated among the governor, the Senate majority leader and the Assembly speaker – long derided as “three men in a room.” (Today, 2 of these people in New York State are now women). Individual state legislators still have power, of course – if you’re among the 20% of Americans who know who they are.

Below the state, the picture thins further. One address can sit in a town, a village inside that town, a county, a school district and a fire district. Each has its own board, and their powers differ from state to state. Local newsrooms have shrunk, so few outlets cover these bodies on a regular basis.

Residents then apply federal rules to bodies where they do not fit. A resident who expects a veto and an override will not find them on most town boards. A resident who expects the mayor to run the schools will find, in most of New York, that the mayor has no role in them.

An array of local government models

The common model of a distinct executive apart from a legislative body is carried by big cities, but doesn’t really carry down to small towns. The “strong-mayor” is the model used by New York City and other large cities; the mayor runs the government; the council passes the budget. Smaller cities and towns have a combined mayor-council system, where the executive has smaller powers to run the council meetings.

No single test separates a strong mayor from a weak one. The National League of Cities describes a continuum of authority, with no sharp line between the two. A strong mayor is usually the chief executive, directs the administration, appoints and removes department heads, and can veto council legislation. In a weak-mayor city the council holds both legislative and executive power (NLC).

Charters mix these features in many combinations. A mayor may appoint department heads but not prepare the budget. A veto may exist and be easy to override. The League of Women Voters of Maine tabulated 16 separate levers a city can raise or lower to shift power among the mayor, the council and a manager (LWV Maine). A list of levers describes a city more accurately than a label does.

Form of government adds a second layer. Most strong mayors serve in mayor-council cities and are elected directly. Most weak mayors serve in council-manager cities, where the mayor is chosen from within the council. Some large cities, such as Phoenix and Dallas, use the council-manager form, so size predicts the form only loosely.

The term also collides with a legal meaning elsewhere. In Ontario, “strong mayor” is a designation the province grants to named municipalities, with defined powers over the chief administrator, the budget and bylaw vetoes (AMCTO). Searches for the US term return those results too.

Municipalities and school districts

In New York, a school district is a separate government from the town, village or city that shares its territory. Most districts are independent, with their own elected boards and budgets that voters approve directly. Their boundaries are often not contiguous with municipal boundaries (State Comptroller). The state had about 700 districts in the mid-2000s.

So one town can be split among several districts, and one district can span parts of several towns. A property tax bill carries lines set by the town, the county and the school district, each by a different body. Neighbors in the same village may sit in different districts and vote for different school boards.

The state also has five types of district: common, union free, central, city and central high school (Department of State). The types date from different eras of state law, and the type affects details such as board size.

There are exceptions. In the five largest cities (Buffalo, New York, Rochester, Syracuse and Yonkers) the school system is part of city government and its budget is part of the city budget (Census Bureau). Those boards are not chosen the same way in each city, and New York City has its own arrangement under state law.

Other states organize schools differently. The Census Bureau counts Maryland, North Carolina, Alaska, Hawaii and DC as having no independent school districts, and Hawaii runs one statewide system (Census Bureau). Florida and West Virginia draw districts on county lines alone (SREB).

A county-style board simplifies the map. A resident has one board to find and one district to learn. It also puts more people under each board member, and decisions about a single school go to a body that covers the whole county. Where the board also depends on another government for its revenue, two bodies share responsibility for one budget (Connecticut OLR).

New York’s arrangement has the opposite problem. Boards are small and close to their communities, and the lines do not match the lines of the other governments residents deal with.

An example: transit oversight

Let’s use a practical example. Most people’s everyday experience with local government in the metropolitan NY area is with transit: subways, buses, and trains. The MTA runs the subways, buses, Long Island Rail Road and Metro-North. Oversight of it runs through at least seven layers, and the layers do not share a parent.

As a train rider, I have occasional questions. How can we get the litter cleaned up by my station? That took 2 years of writing reps at all layers; along the way I discovered that the Permanent Citizens Advisory Committee, which I hadn’t known existed. Can the congestion pricing income fund more late night trains for the Broadway musicians so they don’t have to drive into the city? Still waiting.\

Layer Body Role
Local and county advisory boards Town, village and county transportation or sustainability committees Advise their own governing boards on stations, parking and bus service. No formal role in MTA decisions.
County executives Executives of Nassau, Suffolk, Westchester, Dutchess, Orange, Rockland and Putnam Each recommends one MTA Board member. The Dutchess, Orange, Rockland and Putnam members cast one collective vote (MTA).
County legislative committees County legislature transportation or budget committees Review county budgets, including any county-run bus service and county contributions
State legislative committees Senate and Assembly transportation committees Hold hearings and write transit law. The Senate leader and Assembly speaker also each recommend a member of the review board below.
Capital Program Review Board Four voting members appointed by the governor, one each on the recommendation of the Senate leader, the Assembly speaker and the NYC mayor Approves or rejects the MTA capital plan. Each member holds a veto, and the legislative leaders used theirs in December 2024 (City & State).
Agency board MTA Board Nominated by the governor, with four seats recommended by the NYC mayor and seven by county executives. Six rotating non-voting seats go to labor and to the riders councils (MTA).
Riders councils Permanent Citizens Advisory Committee, with the LIRR, Metro-North and NYC Transit councils Created by the Legislature in 1981. Each council holds one non-voting MTA Board seat. Members are appointed by the governor on recommendations from county executives, borough presidents, the mayor and the public advocate. The MTA budget funds the PCAC (PCAC).

The governor’s column is full. The Senate leader and Assembly speaker each appear once, on the review board. The bodies also link to each other: the MTA Board sends its capital plan to the review board, and each riders council holds a non-voting seat on the MTA Board.

Three things stand out. The governor appears in the appointment chain of the MTA Board, the review board and the riders councils. The body that speaks for riders is appointed by the governor and funded from the MTA’s own budget, though PCAC states that its staff are independent under an agreement with the MTA. And the review board holds a veto over the capital plan while getting little public attention. City & State called it little known and largely opaque.

Bus systems run by counties, and agencies such as NJ Transit and the Port Authority, sit outside this walk-through. Each would add its own layers.

What GovDex Does

Each example above is public information that is hard to assemble. The school district’s board, the members of the MTA Board and the review board that can veto a capital plan are all posted somewhere. Very little of it sits in one place organized around where you live.

GovDex is a directory of the bodies that govern a place and the people who sit on them. It starts with New York. For a locality it lists the town or city, the county, the school district and the smaller boards beneath them, with the person in each seat and how to reach them. Structure differs from state to state, so GovDex records how each body is set up and does not assume the federal pattern.

A resident who can find the right body can decide what to do next: write, attend a meeting, or join an advisory board. Without that, the work goes to people who are paid to know.
